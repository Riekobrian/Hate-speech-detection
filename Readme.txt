### Hate Speech Detection in Kenya

#### 1. The Challenge of Language Understanding in Computers
Computers lack the cognitive ability to understand human language as we do unless they are specifically trained. Human communication is nuanced, context-dependent, and often emotionally charged—qualities that machines don’t naturally grasp. This project aims to bridge that gap by training systems to detect hate speech in Kenyan tweets, a task made complex by the unique linguistic landscape of the country.

#### 2. Hate Speech vs. Freedom of Speech
Hate speech detection must navigate a delicate balance with freedom of speech. In an ideal world, efforts to curb hate speech might "offend both sides equally"—meaning they neither overly restrict expression nor allow harmful rhetoric to flourish unchecked. In Kenya, this balance is particularly challenging due to diverse cultural perspectives and the lack of a universal definition of hate speech, which complicates enforcement and detection efforts.

#### 3. Code-Switching in Kenyan Tweets
In Kenya, tweets on Twitter (now often referred to as X) are frequently code-switched, meaning they blend multiple languages within a single message. People commonly mix English (an official language), Swahili (the national language), and vernacular languages from Kenya’s 42 recognized tribes—plus emerging dialects from new communities. This linguistic diversity reflects Kenya’s rich cultural tapestry but poses a significant hurdle for automated hate speech detection, as systems must recognize and interpret these mixed-language patterns accurately.

#### 4. Defining Hate Speech: A Lack of Consensus
There is no universally agreed-upon definition of hate speech. Different entities—Kenyan laws, international bodies, corporations, and human rights organizations—offer varying interpretations:
- **Kenyan Constitution:** Article 33 guarantees freedom of expression but limits it by prohibiting hate speech, propaganda for war, and incitement to violence. The National Cohesion and Integration Commission (NCIC) further defines hate speech as language that discriminates or incites against protected groups (e.g., based on ethnicity or religion).
- **Kenya Human Rights Bodies:** Groups like the Kenya National Commission on Human Rights (KNCHR) monitor hate speech, often focusing on its role in ethnic tensions, especially during elections.
- **International Bodies:** The United Nations defines hate speech as communication that attacks or discriminates against a person or group based on attributes like race, religion, or ethnicity, but enforcement varies globally.
- **Corporations (e.g., Twitter/X):** Social media platforms have their own policies, often broader or narrower than legal definitions, shaped by global standards and user pressure.

This inconsistency complicates detection efforts, as what’s flagged as hate speech in one context might be permissible in another.

#### 5. The Goal: Combating Hate Speech and Misinformation in Kenya
Can we eliminate hate speech on platforms like Twitter/X, particularly in Kenya? Can we effectively fight hate speech and misinformation? That’s what this project aims to achieve. By developing tools to detect and mitigate hate speech in Kenya’s multilingual, code-switched tweets, we seek to reduce its potential to fuel conflict, especially given Kenya’s history of election-related violence linked to inflammatory rhetoric. Success hinges on training systems to understand Kenya’s linguistic complexity and aligning detection with a practical, context-sensitive definition of hate speech.
